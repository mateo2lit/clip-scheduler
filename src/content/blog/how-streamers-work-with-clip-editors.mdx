At some point, clipping your own streams stops scaling. You stream for four hours, then spend two more finding moments, cutting them vertical, writing captions and posting them to five platforms. The streamers who grow fastest off-stream usually hand that job to a clip editor.

Doing that well comes down to three things: telling your editor which moments matter, giving them what they need without handing over your accounts, and keeping the final say on what goes out under your name.

## What a Clip Editor Actually Does

A good clipper handles most of the pipeline between your stream ending and clips going live:

- **Finds the moments.** Watches the VOD or uses your markers, and picks what's worth cutting.
- **Cuts for each platform.** Vertical for TikTok, Shorts and Reels; tight hooks in the first second.
- **Adds captions and text.** Subtitles, on-screen hooks, sometimes simple effects.
- **Writes the post copy.** Titles and captions that fit each platform.
- **Schedules everything.** Spreads clips across the week instead of dumping them all at once.

What you keep: the final say on anything that goes out under your name.

## Step 1: Tell Your Editor Where the Moments Are

Your editor wasn't in chat. The single biggest time-saver is pointing them at the good parts:

- **Drop markers while you stream.** Twitch stream markers (or a quick note in a shared doc) with a timestamp and one line: "1:42:10, the clutch in round 12".
- **Say what makes it good.** "Start on the jump, not the kill" saves a revision.
- **Share your hit list after each stream.** Three to five moments is plenty.

Timestamps like `1:42:10–1:43:00` are more useful than "the funny bit near the end".

## Step 2: Hand Off Without Handing Over Your Accounts

The worst version of working with an editor is giving them your TikTok, YouTube and Instagram passwords. It's a security risk, it can trip platforms' login-protection checks, and when you part ways you have to change everything.

Better options:

- **Use a scheduler with team access.** You connect your accounts once through each platform's official login; your editor posts through them without ever seeing a password. In [Clip Dash](/), the Team plan lets you invite up to five people who can schedule to your connected accounts.
- **Share media through a folder, not your accounts.** VODs and raw clips in a shared drive, finished clips back the same way.
- **Remove access in one click when the work ends,** instead of changing every password.

## Step 3: Give Them a Style Guide

Consistency is what makes your clips recognizable. A one-page guide covers most of it:

- **Caption style:** all caps or not, emoji or not, your usual sign-off.
- **Hashtags:** the handful you always use, per platform.
- **Hooks:** examples of openers that worked for you.
- **No-go list:** topics, people or games you don't want clipped.
- **Posting rhythm:** how many clips per day, and which platforms get what.

Saving your usual captions and hashtags somewhere your editor can reuse them keeps every post on-brand without you rewriting them.

## Step 4: Keep the Final Say

Especially early on, look at clips before they go out. A simple routine:

1. Your editor schedules clips a day or two ahead.
2. You skim the queue once a day and pull anything you don't like.
3. Give feedback on the clip itself ("start two seconds earlier"), not in a DM thread that gets lost.

Once you trust each other, you can loosen this to spot checks.

## Paying a Clip Editor

Common setups for streamers:

- **Per clip.** Simple to start with. Agree on what counts as a clip (one vertical cut, captioned, posted everywhere).
- **Monthly retainer.** A set number of clips per week for a flat fee. Better for consistency.
- **Revenue share or bonuses.** Some streamers add a bonus when a clip passes a view milestone. Just remember views depend a lot on the moment itself, not only the edit.

Whatever you choose, write it down: what's delivered, how often, and how you'll pay.

## The Bottom Line

A clip editor turns every stream into a week of content, but only if the handoff is smooth. Point them at the moments, give them posting access without your passwords, write down your style, and keep the final say. That's the whole system.
